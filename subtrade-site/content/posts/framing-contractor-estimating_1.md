Framing estimates lose money in two predictable places: the labour to lay out and stand the walls, and everything that lives inside the openings, headers, and assemblies that a footage price quietly ignores. The material is the easy half. Studs, track, and sheathing come off a takeoff and a supplier quote. What separates a framing bid that holds from one that bleeds is how well you convert a wall type and a height onto a drawing into installed man-hours, using rates that came from your own finished jobs rather than a number you remember from a good week three years ago.

## Start with the takeoff, in the units the work is actually built in

A framing takeoff has a few distinct buckets, and lumping them into one "square foot of wall" number is where accuracy dies.

**Wall runs** are measured in linear feet, but never as a single pile. Split them by wall type, by stud gauge or lumber size, by stud spacing, and above all by height. A ten-foot demising wall at 16-inch centres is a different animal from a twenty-two-foot shaft wall or a full-height wall to a deck with deflection track. A linear foot is not a linear foot until you have told it how tall it is and what it does.

**Openings and framed elements** are counted as their own line: door openings, borrowed lights, window openings, headers, sills, and the extra studs, jambs, and blocking that each one eats. Openings are the single most underpriced part of most framing bids, because a footage price treats a wall full of doors the same as a blank corridor.

**Sheathing, blocking, and backing** get counted by area and by count: exterior sheathing by the square foot, plus in-wall blocking for millwork, grab bars, TVs, handrails, and equipment. Backing rarely shows cleanly on the architectural, so it hides until the field asks for it.

Get the quantities right first, in their natural units, before a dollar goes on the sheet. An estimate that starts from a lump-sum cost per square foot of floor plate is a guess wearing a tool belt.

## Price the labour from your own production rates

This is the whole game. Material pricing is arithmetic. Labour pricing is judgement, and the only judgement worth trusting is your own historical production rate, measured on jobs you actually finished and got paid for.

A production rate is simply how long a unit of work takes your crew: man-hours per hundred linear feet of a given wall type at a given height, man-hours per framed opening, man-hours per hundred square feet of sheathing. Once you have them, the estimate builds itself. Quantity times rate equals hours, hours times your burdened labour rate equals cost.

The trap is borrowing someone else's rate. A generic table says a metal stud partition frames at some tidy number per linear foot. Maybe it does, on the ground floor of an empty shell with a clear layout line. Your job is level four, the deck is out of level, half the walls die into existing structure, and your real rate was well off the table. The generic number underbids you, you win the work, and you lose the margin.

| Scope item | Natural unit | What drives the labour rate |
|---|---|---|
| Partition walls | linear ft | Height, gauge, spacing, layout complexity |
| Full-height and shaft walls | linear ft | Deck height, deflection track, bracing, access |
| Framed openings | each | Header size, jamb studs, blocking, door type |
| Sheathing | sq ft | Height, fastener schedule, weather, edge detail |
| In-wall blocking and backing | each or linear ft | Location, coordination, ceiling access |

The rate from your own history already carries the layout friction, the out-of-level deck, and the crew you actually run. That is why it beats any published table.

## Where framing estimates go wrong

### Height gets averaged instead of split

Standing a twelve-foot wall costs more per linear foot than standing an eight-foot wall, and not by a little once staging and a second row of bracing enter the picture. Estimators who carry one blended footage rate across a building with three ceiling heights overprice the low walls and badly underprice the tall ones, which are usually the ones that hurt.

### Openings are treated as free footage

Every door, borrowed light, and window is extra studs, a header, cripples, and layout time, and it removes almost no wall to offset it. A corridor of offices with a door every twelve feet is far more labour per foot than a warehouse demising wall. Count the openings, apply a real per-opening rate, and stop letting a footage number swallow them.

### Deflection, blocking, and backing go missing

Deflection track at the deck, fire and sound assemblies, and in-wall blocking for everything the other trades hang later are real hours that rarely jump off the architectural drawings. If your production rates come from your own jobs that included all of it, the friction is already baked in. If they come from a clean table, it is not, and the field eats the difference.

### Nobody prices the extras

The biggest single leak in framing margin is not the estimate at all, it is the [unsigned change order](/blog/unsigned-change-order-recovery/). A wall moved after layout, added backing the owner wanted late, a revised opening, a fire-rated assembly swapped in on a bulletin. Each one is billable if you document it the day it happens and price it from the same labour rates you bid with. Lose the paper trail and it turns into free work. Tracking those extras against the original scope is the same discipline as clean [job costing](/blog/job-costing-for-subcontractors/): you cannot recover what you never recorded.

## Turn the estimate into a job you can measure

An estimate is only worth what you learn from it. The reason to price labour by production rate is that after the job you can compare estimated hours to actual hours, cost code by cost code, and correct the rate before the next bid. That loop, estimate to actual and back, is how a framing shop gets sharper every quarter instead of guessing forever.

It only works if the field data comes back clean. Crew [time tracking](/time-tracking/) coded to the same cost codes you estimated against is what closes the loop. Without it, your production rates are just the last number that felt good. With it, every finished job makes the next bid more accurate.

## A short pre-bid checklist

1. Take off wall runs in linear feet, split by type, gauge or lumber size, spacing, and height.
2. Count openings, headers, and framed elements as their own line, each.
3. Count sheathing by area and blocking or backing by count and location.
4. Apply your own production rates, not a table, to turn quantities into hours.
5. Add layout, staging, and bracing time explicitly for tall and full-height walls.
6. Load the hours at your real burdened rate, including travel and non-productive time.
7. Set up the cost codes so the field reports actuals against the same structure you bid.

## FAQ

**How do framing contractors estimate labour accurately?**

By measuring production rates from their own completed jobs, man-hours per unit of wall, opening, and sheathing, then multiplying quantity by rate by burdened labour cost. Rates from your own work beat any generic table because they already reflect your crews, your typical layouts, and the access conditions you actually face.

**Should I estimate framing by the linear foot or the square foot?**

Linear foot of wall, split by height and type, tracks framing labour far better than square foot of floor. Square foot of floor plate hides height, opening density, and assembly type, which are the variables that actually move your hours, so keep it for early budgets only.

**What is the most common framing estimating mistake?**

Carrying one blended footage rate across walls of different heights and opening densities. The tall walls and the door-heavy corridors get underpriced, and those are usually the walls that decide whether the job made money.

**How is framing estimating different from framing contractor software?**

Estimating is the pricing method: takeoff, production rates, labour cost. Software is the system that stores your historical rates, feeds field actuals back against them, and tracks the change orders that protect the margin. Good estimating needs both a method and the data to feed it.

**How do I keep my framing production rates current?**

Compare estimated hours to actual hours on every finished job, by cost code, and adjust. This needs crew time tracking coded the same way you estimated. Do it every job and your rates drift toward reality instead of wishful thinking.

**Can I price framing by cost per square foot of building?**

Only for the roughest early budget. A square-foot number hides height, opening count, assembly type, and access, so it will be wrong on any specific job. Use it for a ballpark, never for a firm bid.
