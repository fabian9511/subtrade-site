The drawing set that matters is not the one in the trailer. It is the one your foreman is standing in front of, the one with the beam that is two inches off, the wall that moved, the backing the owner added on a walk. Those field markups are where a project actually gets built, and on most jobs they live on a coffee-stained paper set that never makes it back to anyone who needs it. When the markups get lost, so does the evidence of what changed, who directed it, and when. That is not a paperwork problem. That is a getting-paid problem.

We built SubTrade inside Quality Gypsum Services, a commercial drywall contractor in Calgary, Alberta, and we have watched clean field markups win a back charge dispute and watched a missing redline hand away a legitimate extra. Here is how to mark up drawings in the field so the information survives the job.

## Why the field set is worth more than the office set

The office holds the contract drawings. The field holds the truth. Every gap between the two, a dimension that did not work, a conflict between trades, a detail that was wrong for the condition, gets resolved on site, usually verbally, usually fast. If nobody captures that resolution on the drawing at the moment it happens, it exists only in the memory of whoever was standing there, and memories do not hold up at closeout or in a dispute.

A field markup does three jobs at once. It tells the next crew what is actually there. It builds the record set you owe at the end of the project. And it timestamps the change so that when the extra work shows up on an invoice, you can point to the exact drawing, the exact date, and the exact instruction behind it.

## Set up one source of truth before the job starts

Most markup chaos traces back to a single failure: more than one current set. The office is on revision 4, the foreman is on revision 2, and the second-floor crew is working off a printout somebody made in week one. Everyone is marking up a different drawing, and none of the markups line up.

Fix it before the first stud goes up. Pick one place the current drawings live, make sure every phone and tablet on the crew opens that same place, and kill the paper sets that compete with it. When a revision lands, the old set has to visibly go away, not sit in a truck as a landmine. A markup on a superseded drawing is worse than no markup, because it looks authoritative and is wrong.

## What actually gets marked up in the field

Not everything belongs on the drawing. Reserve the markup for information that a future reader needs to see against the geometry.

### Redlines: what changed from the drawing

The core of field markup. A wall relocated, a dimension corrected, a detail built differently than shown because the field condition demanded it. Draw it on the drawing in a colour that reads as a change, note why in a few words, and date it.

### As-builts: what is now buried

Anything that gets covered up needs to be recorded before it disappears: in-wall backing and blocking, the actual routing of services, the real location of an access panel. Once the board or the finish goes on, the only record of what is behind it is the markup you made and the [photo you took](/blog/jobsite-photo-documentation/) beside it. Pair the two and the buried condition is documented for good.

### Coordination conflicts and RFI locations

When two trades want the same space, mark the conflict on the drawing where it occurs and tie it to the RFI number. A cloud around the conflict with a note beats a paragraph in an email that nobody can locate against the plan three weeks later.

### Directed changes and added scope

When someone tells you to build something not on the drawings, the markup is your first line of evidence. Cloud the area, note who directed it and when, and it becomes the anchor for the change order that follows.

## Make the markup a record, not a memory

A red line with no context is only half a record. Three things turn a markup into evidence: a date, a name, and a photo. Who marked it, when, and what the condition looked like at the time. On paper, those three things are almost never all present. On a phone, they can be automatic, the timestamp and the author ride along with the file, and a [site photo](/construction-management-features/site-photos/) attaches to the same location on the same drawing.

This is where field markups and the [daily log](/construction-management-features/daily-logs/) reinforce each other. The log says the crew was on that scope that day. The markup shows what they found and what changed. The photo proves the condition. Together they are very hard to argue with, and that is exactly the point.

## Version control is the part that quietly kills you

Paper cannot do version control, and that is its fatal flaw as a markup medium. When revision 5 supersedes revision 4, every markup on the paper 4 has to be carried forward by hand or it is lost. Nobody does that reliably under production pressure, so markups evaporate at every revision.

A digital set carries the markup with the drawing and shows which revision it was made against. When a new revision lands, you can see what was marked on the old one and decide, deliberately, what still applies. Contract drawings and specifications are contract documents in their own right; the standard [CCDC contracts](https://www.ccdc.org/documents/) treat the drawing set as part of the binding agreement, which is exactly why the revision your crew worked to, and what they marked on it, is worth keeping straight.

| | Paper markup | Digital field markup |
|---|---|---|
| Date and author | Rarely captured | Automatic |
| Photo tied to location | Separate, gets lost | Attached to the drawing |
| Survives a revision | Re-drawn by hand or lost | Carried and version-tagged |
| Reaches the office | When someone remembers | Immediately |
| Available at closeout | If the set survives | Always in the record |

## From markup to money

The reason to get this right is not tidiness. It is recovery. The two places field markups pay for themselves are change orders and back charges.

On the change order side, a dated, attributed markup is the difference between a signed extra and an argument. The extra work has to be documented the day it happens, and the drawing is where it lands most clearly. On the [back charge](/blog/construction-back-charges/) side, when someone tries to charge you for damage or rework, your marked-up set showing the condition you actually left is your defence. Either way, the marked drawing is evidence, and evidence is what gets paid.

## A field markup routine that takes two minutes

1. Open the current set, confirm it is the current revision, and work only on that.
2. When a condition differs from the drawing, mark it immediately, in the field, not at the end of the day from memory.
3. Use a consistent colour or style for changes so a redline reads as a redline at a glance.
4. Add a short note: what changed and why, in a few words.
5. Attach a photo of the condition to the same spot on the drawing.
6. Let the date and your name ride with the markup automatically.
7. When a revision lands, review the old markups and carry forward the ones that still apply.

## FAQ

**What is a construction drawing markup?**

A markup is any change, note, or clarification drawn directly onto a construction drawing to show how the work differs from, or adds to, what was originally documented. In the field it captures redlines, as-built conditions, coordination conflicts, and directed changes against the actual geometry of the plan.

**What is the difference between a redline and an as-built?**

A redline records a change from the design intent, a wall moved, a dimension corrected. An as-built records the final installed condition, especially anything that gets covered up. Redlines are made during the work; the collected as-built set is what you hand over at closeout.

**Why are field markups better on a phone or tablet than on paper?**

Digital markups automatically carry the date and author, attach photos to the exact location, reach the office immediately, and survive drawing revisions instead of being lost each time a new set is issued. Paper markups depend on someone re-drawing them by hand and physically returning the set.

**How do drawing markups help me get paid?**

A dated, attributed markup is direct evidence for a change order and a defence against a back charge. When you can show the exact drawing, the date, and who directed the work, a disputed extra becomes a documented one, and documented extras get paid.

**Who should be marking up drawings on site?**

Whoever is closest to the condition, usually the foreman or lead hand, at the moment the difference is found. The goal is capture at the source, not a clean copy transcribed later, because the details and the reasons fade fast once the crew moves on.

**How do I keep everyone on the current drawing revision?**

Keep one source of truth that every device opens, make superseded sets visibly disappear when a revision lands, and avoid loose paper printouts that compete with the live set. One current set, marked by everyone, is the whole objective.
