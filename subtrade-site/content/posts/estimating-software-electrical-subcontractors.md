Electrical estimating breaks most general construction software because the unit of work is wrong. A drywall estimator thinks in square feet of board. A concrete estimator thinks in cubic metres. An electrical estimator thinks in *counts* — devices, fixtures, homeruns, panels, feet of conduit and wire — and every one of those counts carries its own labour unit. A tool that only understands area understands almost nothing about pricing electrical work.

That mismatch is why so many electrical subs still bid off a spreadsheet and a gut feel. This is how electrical estimating software actually works, and what separates a tool built for the trade from a generic platform with the word "electrical" on the pricing page. If you're comparing products rather than method, our [electrical contractor software](/electrical-contractor-software/) page covers the field side; this one is about the estimate.

## Why electrical estimating is its own animal

An electrical takeoff is a counting exercise before it's anything else. You go through the drawings and the panel schedule and you tally: how many duplex receptacles, how many single-pole switches, how many 2x4 troffers, how many homeruns back to which panel, how many feet of each conduit size and wire gauge. Nothing about that maps to square footage. Two rooms the same size can differ threefold in device density and price accordingly.

Then each count turns into labour and material through a *unit* — the hours and dollars it takes to install one of that item, rough and finish. A receptacle isn't one line; it's a box, a device, a plate, a length of wire, a share of the homerun, and the labour to rough it in and trim it out. Estimating electrical work well means holding thousands of those little bundles and pricing them consistently, job after job.

## The count-and-assembly method

Good electrical estimating software is built around two ideas: counting from the drawings, and pricing through assemblies.

### Count once, cleanly

The takeoff is where estimates are won or lost on time. Counting devices by hand off paper drawings is slow and easy to double-count or miss. On-screen takeoff — dropping a count marker on each symbol on the digital drawing — is faster, auditable, and lets you recount a revised sheet without starting over. The output is a clean tally by type and by area.

### Price through assemblies, not line items

This is the part that matters. An **assembly** is a pre-built bundle for a common install — say, a duplex receptacle: box, device, plate, 20 feet of 12 AWG, connectors, and the rough plus finish labour. You build the assembly once, with your own material costs and your own labour units, and from then on every receptacle you count pulls the whole bundle in automatically.

Estimate a 200-device tenant fit-up and the difference is stark. Line-by-line, that's a thousand entries and a long afternoon. Through assemblies, it's a count that expands into a priced estimate in minutes, and every receptacle is priced identically to the last job — no drift, no forgotten wire nut.

## Labour units and your own production rates

Material pricing is the easy half — suppliers publish it. Labour is where the money is made or lost, and where generic tools are weakest. Many electrical estimators start from published labour units (the NECA *Manual of Labor Units* is the common reference). That's a fine starting point. It is not your shop.

Your crews, on your kind of work, in your buildings, don't install at the book rate — they install at *your* rate, and only your completed jobs know what that is. The estimate that beats the field database is the one fed by your own history: what a homerun actually took your team last quarter, not what a national average predicts. We treat that production-rate feedback as the heart of estimating generally, and it's just as true in electrical as it is in [drywall estimating](/blog/drywall-estimating-software/) — the units differ, the loop is the same.

## Where generic software falls down

A platform built for general contractors, or a one-size spreadsheet, tends to fail electrical estimating in three predictable ways.

It has no concept of an assembly, so you price every device as a naked line item and the estimate takes all day. It measures in area or lump sum, so it can't hold device counts or homerun lengths as first-class quantities. And it has no path from the finished job back to the estimate, so your labour units never improve — you bid the book rate forever, whether or not your crews beat it.

None of those are cosmetic. They're the difference between an estimate you trust and a number you hope is right.

## FAQ

**What is electrical estimating software?**

It's a tool for pricing electrical work by counting devices, fixtures, homeruns and conduit off the drawings and expanding those counts into priced material and labour through pre-built assemblies, rather than pricing every item as a separate line.

**Why can't I just use general construction estimating software?**

Most general tools measure in area or lump sums and have no assembly logic, so they can't hold device counts or price a homerun the way electrical work is actually built. You end up entering thousands of line items by hand and the labour units never reflect your own crews.

**What is an assembly in electrical estimating?**

A bundle representing one complete install — a receptacle, for example, as its box, device, plate, wire, connectors and rough-plus-finish labour. You build it once, and every count of that item pulls the whole priced bundle in automatically.

**Should I use NECA labour units or my own?**

Published units like NECA's are a reasonable starting point, but your own completed-job history is more accurate for your crews and your buildings. The best setups start from a reference and then tune the units from actual installed hours.

**How does estimating software improve my bids over time?**

By comparing each estimate against the actual labour the job consumed. When field time tracking feeds installed cost back to the office, your labour units get corrected job by job, so every bid is grounded in your real production rather than a static average.

**Does it work for both bid-build and design-build electrical work?**

Yes. The count-and-assembly method prices a hard bid off finished drawings and, with adjustable assemblies and your own unit costs, supports the iterative pricing a design-build or negotiated job needs.
